    Vascular Disease: Introduction

    Core idea: Vascular disorders cause more morbidity and mortality than any other category of human diseases, through two principal mechanisms: narrowing/obstruction of lumens and weakening of vessel walls.

    1. Importance of vascular disorders

    Vascular disorders are responsible for more morbidity and mortality than any other category of human diseases.

    2. Two principal mechanisms of vascular disease

    Two main principal mechanisms underlie vascular disease:

    The two mechanisms 2 rows
    MechanismDescriptionExamples
    1. Narrowing (stenosis) or complete obstruction of vessel lumensProgressively — e.g., by atherosclerosis.
    Abruptly — e.g., by thrombosis or embolism.    		Atherosclerosis; thrombosis; embolism
    2. Weakening of vessel wallsLeading to dilation or rupture.Aneurysm, dissection

    Key point. Lumen problem (stenosis / obstruction) vs wall problem (weakening → dilation or rupture).

    Quick Review

    • Vascular disorders → more morbidity and mortality than any other category of human diseases.
    • Mechanism 1: narrowing (stenosis) or complete obstruction — progressively (atherosclerosis) or abruptly (thrombosis or embolism).
    • Mechanism 2: weakening of vessel walls → dilation or rupture (aneurysm, dissection).

    Normal Histology and Anatomy of the Vessel Wall

    Core idea: The blood-vessel wall is built from cells (ECs and SMCs) and extracellular matrix, arranged into three layers from lumen to outside: intima, media and adventitia.

    1. Basic components of the wall

    Basic components of the wall of blood vessels include:

    1. Endothelial Cells (ECs) and smooth Muscle Cells (SMCs).
    2. Extracellular Matrix (ECM).

    2. The three layers (from lumen to outside)

    These components are arranged into three layers (from lumen to outside):

    1. Intima
      single layer of endothelial cells
    2. Media
      smooth muscle cells
    3. Adventitia
      connective tissue, vasa vasorum, nerve fibers

    Unit 3 of 7

    Classification of Arteries

    Core idea: According to their size & structure, arteries are classified into large (elastic), medium-sized (muscular), and small arteries and arterioles.

    1. Three classes of arteries

    According to their size & structure, arteries can be classified into the following:

    Classes, examples and media 3 classes
    ClassExamples / sizeMedia
    (1) Large or elastic arteriesThe aorta, the major branches of the aorta (subclavian, common carotid, and iliac), and pulmonary arteries.Elastic fiber-rich media (arranged in layers of lamellar units of elastin fibers and SMCs like tree rings).
    (2) Medium-sized or muscular arteriesSmaller branches of the aorta (e.g., coronary and renal arteries).Composed predominantly of SMC.
    (3) Small arteries and arteriolesSmall arteries (≤2 mm in diameter) and arterioles (20 to 100 µm in diameter) within tissues and organs.Consists of smooth muscles; their contraction causes changes in diameter that regulate systemic arterial blood pressure.

    2. Arterioles and blood pressure

    The media of small arteries and arterioles consists of smooth muscles, and their contraction causes changes in diameter that regulate systemic arterial blood pressure.

    Key point. So the arterioles are the principals' points of physiologic resistance (control the blood pressure).

    Quick Review

    • Elastic: aorta, subclavian, common carotid, iliac, pulmonary — elastic fiber-rich media (lamellar units like tree rings).
    • Muscular: coronary, renal — media predominantly SMC.
    • Small arteries ≤2 mm; arterioles 20–100 µm — smooth-muscle contraction regulates systemic arterial blood pressure; arterioles = main points of physiologic resistance.

    Unit 4 of 7

    Atherosclerosis: Definition, Targets and Consequences

    Core idea: Atherosclerosis is thickening/hardening of arteries due to atheromatous plaque in the inner lining; it targets vital arteries, causing roughly half of all deaths.

    1. Definition

    Atherosclerosis is the thickening or hardening of the arteries caused by a buildup of atheromatous plaque (atheroma) in the inner lining of an artery.

    2. Importance and target arteries

    This disease is responsible for more deaths and serious complications than any other disorder (roughly half of all deaths). This is because its prime targets are vital arteries, namely the coronaries, cerebral arteries, the aorta.

    Arteries primarily affected 2 types

    Atherosclerosis is “a disease primarily of large elastic arteries (aorta, carotid, and the iliac arteries) and medium-sized muscular arteries (coronaries and popliteal arteries).

    Artery typeExamples affected
    Large elastic arteriesAorta, carotid, iliac arteries
    Medium-sized muscular arteriesCoronaries, popliteal arteries

    3. Atheromatous plaque

    Atheromatous plaques are raised lesions composed of soft grumous lipid cores (mainly cholesterol and cholesterol esters, with necrotic debris) covered by fibrous caps.

    Plaque composition 2 parts
    PartComposition
    CoreSoft grumous lipid core — mainly cholesterol and cholesterol esters, with necrotic debris.
    CoverFibrous cap.

    Figure — Normal vs narrowed artery. Left: Artery with normal blood flow. Right: Artery narrowed by atherosclerosis — blood flow reduced by plaque.

    4. Major consequences

    Its major consequences are:

    1. Myocardial infarction (alone responsible for about 25% of all deaths).
    2. Cerebral infarction.
    3. Aortic aneurysm.

    Key point. Atherosclerosis ≈ half of all deaths; myocardial infarction alone ≈ 25% of all deaths.

    Quick Review

    • Definition: thickening or hardening of arteries from atheromatous plaque (atheroma) in the inner lining.
    • Prime targets: coronaries, cerebral arteries, aorta → roughly half of all deaths.
    • Primarily large elastic (aorta, carotid, iliac) and medium-sized muscular (coronaries, popliteal) arteries.
    • Plaque: raised lesion; soft grumous lipid core (cholesterol, cholesterol esters, necrotic debris) + fibrous cap.
    • Consequences: myocardial infarction (~25% of all deaths), cerebral infarction, aortic aneurysm.

    Unit 6 of 7

    Pathogenesis: Response to Injury Hypothesis

    Core idea: The most widely accepted theory — the response to injury hypothesis — views atherosclerosis as a chronic inflammatory and healing response of the arterial wall to endothelial injury.

    1. Historical theories of atherogenesis

    Historically, there have been two dominant theories regarding atherogenesis:

    Two historical theories 2 theories
    TheoryFocus
    The firstAn intimal cellular proliferation in response to endothelial injury.
    The otherRepeated formation and organization of thrombi.

    2. The response to injury hypothesis

    The most widely accepted theory of pathogenesis is called the response to injury hypothesis. This theory incorporates elements of both theories and also integrates the risk factors.

    This theory states that atherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory and healing response of the arterial wall to endothelial injury.

    3. According to this model, atherosclerosis results from the following

    1. Endothelial injury—and resultant endothelial dysfunction—leading to increased permeability, leukocyte adhesion, and thrombosis.
    2. Accumulation of lipoproteins (mainly oxidized LDL and cholesterol crystals) in the vessel wall.
    3. Platelet adhesion.
    4. Monocyte adhesion to the endothelium, migration into the intima, and differentiation into macrophages and foam cells.
    5. Lipid accumulation within macrophages, which release inflammatory cytokines.
    6. Smooth muscle cell recruitment due to factors released from activated platelets, macrophages, and vascular wall cells.
    7. Smooth muscle cell proliferation and ECM production.
    Figure — Atherosclerosis progression labels

    Figure — Atherosclerosis progression. Layers shown: Lumen, Intima, Media. LDL in the lumen enters the intima; m-LDL with ↑ SMase (SM → Cer) undergoes aggregation. Monocytes enter the intima and become macrophages, which take up the aggregated lipid and become foam cells. Foam cells lead to plaque formation, with stenosis of the lumen.

    Key point. Response to injury = both historical theories + the risk factors; atherosclerosis = chronic inflammatory and healing response to endothelial injury.

    Quick Review

    • Old theories: intimal cellular proliferation in response to endothelial injury; repeated formation and organization of thrombi.
    • Response to injury hypothesis: most widely accepted; combines both theories and integrates risk factors.
    • Results from: endothelial injury/dysfunction, lipoprotein accumulation (oxidized LDL, cholesterol crystals), platelet adhesion, monocyte adhesion → macrophages and foam cells, lipid in macrophages → cytokines, SMC recruitment, SMC proliferation and ECM production.

    Unit 7 of 7

    Endothelial Injury and Stages of Atherogenesis

    Core idea: Endothelial injury is the cornerstone of the response to injury hypothesis; the response proceeds in five stages ending in a well-developed plaque.

    1. Endothelial injury

    • Endothelial cell injury is the cornerstone of the response to injury hypothesis.
    • Endothelial cell loss due to any kind of injury.
    • Suspected triggers of early atheromatous lesions include hypertension, hyperlipidemia, toxins from cigarette smoke, homocysteine, and even infectious agents.
    • Inflammatory cytokines (e.g., tumor necrosis factor [TNF]) also can stimulate proatherogenic patterns of endothelial cell gene expression.

    2. The most important causes of endothelial injury

    1. Hemodynamic disturbances
    2. Hypercholesterolemia
    3. Inflammation

    • Endothelial cell injury = cornerstone of the response to injury hypothesis; endothelial cell loss due to any kind of injury.

    • Most important causes: hemodynamic disturbances, hypercholesterolemia, inflammation.
    • Figure stages: fatty streak (stage 3) → fibrofatty atheroma (stage 4) → collagen, lipid debris (stage 5).
